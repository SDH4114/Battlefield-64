Starting Position

The chessboard consists of 64 squares (8×8), alternating light and dark colors. Important: the light square must always be in the lower right corner from each player's perspective (the "light on right" rule).

Piece Placement:

  • 1st rank (for White a1–h1): Rook, Knight, Bishop, Queen, King, Bishop, Knight, Rook
  • 2nd rank: All pawns
  • The queen: always starts on a square of her own color (white queen on a light square, black queen on a dark square)
  • The king: goes on the remaining central square

White always moves first! Players alternate moves and may move only one piece per turn (except when castling).

Pieces and Their Moves

King (♔)

Moves: one square in any direction (horizontally, vertically, or diagonally).

Key points:

  • The most important piece — losing the king means losing the game (checkmate)
  • Cannot move to a square that is under attack
  • Cannot stand on a square adjacent to the enemy king
  • Participates in castling

Queen (♕)

Moves: any number of squares along ranks, files, or diagonals (combines rook and bishop movement).

Key points:

  • Strongest attacking piece on the board
  • From the center can control up to 27 squares
  • Cannot jump over other pieces
  • Pawns usually promote to a queen

Rook (♖)

Moves: any number of squares horizontally or vertically (straight lines).

Key points:

  • Participates in castling
  • Considered a heavy piece
  • Starts in the corners of the board
  • Cannot jump over other pieces

Bishop (♗)

Moves: any number of squares diagonally.

Key points:

  • Always remains on the same color squares
  • Can be light-squared or dark-squared
  • Each player starts with two bishops on opposite colors
  • Cannot jump over other pieces

Knight (♘)

Moves: in an L-shape — two squares in one direction and then one square perpendicular.

Key points:

  • The only piece that can jump over other pieces
  • Changes square color with every move
  • You cannot block a knight's attack with another piece
  • Especially powerful in closed positions

Pawn (♙)

Moves: one square forward (from its starting rank it may move two squares forward).

Captures: one square diagonally forward.

Key points:

  • Cannot move backward
  • Cannot capture pieces directly in front of it
  • Upon reaching the last rank, must promote to another piece (except a king)
  • Can participate in en passant captures

Special Moves

Castling

The only move where two pieces move at the same time — the king and a rook.

How it works:

  • King-side castling (O-O): the king moves two squares toward the rook on the king side, and that rook moves to the square next to the king
  • Queen-side castling (O-O-O): the same idea, but with the rook on the queen side

Conditions for castling:

  • The king and the rook involved have not moved earlier in the game
  • No pieces between the king and that rook
  • The king is not currently in check
  • The king does not move through a square that is under attack
  • The king does not end up in check after castling

En Passant

A special pawn capture.

When it is possible:

  • An enemy pawn moves two squares forward from its starting rank
  • It lands on a square adjacent to your pawn (next file)
  • Your pawn may capture it as if it had moved only one square forward
Important: En passant is only available on the very next move after the enemy pawn makes the two-square advance. If you do not capture immediately, the opportunity is lost.

Pawn Promotion

When a pawn reaches the last rank (8th rank for White, 1st rank for Black), it must be promoted to another piece chosen by the player:

  • Queen (the most common choice)
  • Rook
  • Bishop
  • Knight
Tip: In the vast majority of cases, promoting to a queen is best because it is the strongest piece. However, sometimes promoting to a knight can create a checkmate or a fork.

Objective of the Game

Check

When the king is under attack (one or more opposing pieces could capture it on the next move), this is called check. The player must immediately respond by:

  • Moving the king to a safe square
  • Blocking the attack with another piece
  • Capturing the attacking piece

Checkmate

Checkmate is a check where there is no legal move to escape. When a king is checkmated, the game ends and the attacking side wins.

Main goal of chess: Deliver checkmate to your opponent's king. The king is never actually taken off the board — the game ends as soon as checkmate occurs.

Draw Rules

A game can end in a draw in several ways:

Stalemate

The player to move is not in check but has no legal moves at all. This is a draw, not a loss.

Threefold Repetition

If the same position occurs three times (not necessarily in a row), the game can be declared a draw.

Fifty-Move Rule

If fifty consecutive moves by both players occur without any pawn move or capture, a draw can be claimed.

Insufficient Material

If neither side has enough material to force checkmate, the game is drawn. For example:

  • King vs king
  • King + bishop vs king
  • King + knight vs king
  • King + bishop vs king + bishop (bishops on the same color squares)

Agreement

The players can agree to a draw at any moment by mutual consent.

Tips for Beginners

Piece Values

Approximate relative value of the pieces (in pawns):

  • Queen: 9 pawns
  • Rook: 5 pawns
  • Bishop: 3 pawns
  • Knight: 3 pawns
  • Pawn: 1 pawn
  • King: priceless!

Core Principles

  • Control the center (e4, d4, e5, d5)
  • Develop your pieces in the opening
  • Castle early to protect your king
  • Do not move the same piece too many times in the opening
  • Always watch your king's safety

Common Beginner Mistakes

  • Bringing the queen out too early
  • Forgetting to castle
  • Ignoring your opponent's threats
  • Trading a strong piece for a weaker one
  • Leaving the king in the center for too long

How to Improve

  • Play regularly — practice is more important than memorizing theory
  • Review your games afterwards to find mistakes
  • Solve tactical puzzles
  • Study basic openings and endgames
  • Watch games by strong players
Golden rule: Before every move, ask yourself: "What is my opponent threatening?" and "What will my move threaten?" This simple habit helps avoid many blunders.
